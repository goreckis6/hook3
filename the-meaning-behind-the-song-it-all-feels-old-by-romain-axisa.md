The first time I heard Romain Axisa’s “It All Feels Old,” I was struck by a paradox that lingers in the song’s quiet chorus: a yearning for something that simultaneously feels fresh and exhausted. The track rides a gently looping synth line while the singer’s voice drifts over it, suggesting both nostalgia and a restless desire to escape the same feeling. That tug‑of‑war—between comfort and claustrophobia, between love that still burns and memories that have begun to rust—makes the song a perfect candidate for a deeper look. Axisa doesn’t spell out a story in blunt terms; instead, he layers ambiguous images, a lyrical structure that turns back on itself, and a production palette that feels deliberately aged. Unpacking those layers reveals a meditation on *time’s erosion of intimacy*, the way affection can become a familiar haunt, and the fragile hope that a new moment can still be found within an old pattern.

## Key Takeaways
- **The song explores the fading edge of intimacy**, where love feels both familiar and stale.  
- **Nostalgia acts as both comfort and cage**, illustrated through recurring images of old photographs and dimly lit rooms.  
- **The title functions as a paradox**, framing the entire narrative as something that is simultaneously new and exhausted.  
- **Production choices—retro synths, lo‑fi vinyl crackle, and spacious reverb—mirror the lyrical theme of age and repetition.**  
- **Listeners connect because the track vocalizes the universal experience of confronting a relationship that has become a comforting habit rather than a living spark.**

## The Emotional Core of “It All Feels Old”

At its heart, “It All Feels Old” is voiced from the perspective of someone standing in a familiar place—a bedroom, a café, a shared apartment—watching the routine they once built together crumble into a predictable loop. The narrator’s voice is hushed, almost tired, which immediately signals *resignation* rather than anger. The emotional palette is built from three primary feelings:

1. **Nostalgic longing** – The narrator reaches back to moments that once felt vibrant, using visual cues like faded photographs or the smell of an old perfume. This longing isn’t purely sentimental; it’s laced with an awareness that those moments are *irretrievable*.
2. **Quiet desperation** – A sense of being trapped within the same walls. The repetitive phrasing (“again, again, again”) suggests a mantra that has turned into an internal chant, emphasizing the feeling of being stuck in a cycle.
3. **Faint hope** – Despite the oppressive sense of déjà vu, there are subtle sonic lifts—a gentle chord change, a brief pause—hinting that the narrator still craves a breakthrough. The hope isn’t loud; it’s more like the faint glow of a streetlamp at night: present, but easily overlooked.

Within these emotional strands the narrative voice maintains an *observational distance* from its own feelings, which is crucial. By describing the scene in almost cinematic terms—“the light through the curtains flickers like a film reel”—the narrator can both witness and critique their own experience, emphasizing the song’s reflective quality.

## Main Themes and Message

### The Weight of Repetition

One of the dominant themes is **the burden of repetition**. The song repeatedly returns to the same lyrical motifs—old songs, repeated gestures, familiar conversations. This creates a lyrical echo chamber that mimics how a long‑term relationship can feel like a series of rehearsed scenes. The message here is not to condemn the repetition itself but to identify how it can erode the immediacy of feeling.

### The Duality of Nostalgia

Nostalgia in “It All Feels Old” functions as both an *anchor* and a *shackle*. The track’s verses evoke images of earlier, more intense moments, which serve as an anchor that prevents the narrator from completely moving on. Simultaneously, those same memories become shackles because they highlight the gap between past intensity and present mundanity. The song subtly asks the listener: **When does nostalgia stop being a refuge and start being a prison?**

### Searching for Renewal Within the Familiar

Even as the track dwells on staleness, there’s an undercurrent that suggests renewal can emerge from familiar terrain. The refrain, though repetitive, rises slightly in pitch each time, hinting at an incremental climb rather than a grand, sudden breakthrough. This reflects a message of *gradual transformation*: the possibility that small, intentional shifts—an altered chord, a different lyric line—can gradually breathe new life into an old structure.

## Symbolism and Metaphors

### “Old” as a Material and a Temporal State

The adjective “old” works on two levels. Materially, it conjures images of worn objects—cracked vinyl, rusted metal, pages yellowed with time. Temporally, it denotes a feeling of *stagnation*. When the narrator says “everything feels old,” the lyric becomes a metaphorical microscope, examining how every sensory input—smell of coffee, the colour of a sunset—has been filtered through the lens of accumulated memory, making the present seem pre‑colored.

### Photographs and Film Reel Imagery

Repeated references to photographs, film reels, and static images draw attention to the *capturing* of moments. Photographs freeze a slice of time, preserving it but also preventing its evolution. By depicting their relationship as a series of images, the song suggests that the narrator is trying to keep love static, which in turn accelerates the sense that everything has become “old.” The metaphor extends to the idea that *memory itself is a curated gallery*, not a living experience.

### The “Light Through the Curtains”

A subtle, recurring visual cue is the light that filters through thin curtains. Light often indicates clarity or revelation in songwriting, but here it is described as flickering, soft, and diffused—suggesting that insight is only partially illuminated. It underscores the notion that the narrator can see faint glimmers of change, yet they are constantly filtered through layers of history.

## The Role of the Title and Hook

The title, **“It All Feels Old,”** functions as both a statement and a question. By casting the entire emotional landscape under a single blanket phrase, Axisa creates a hook that invites listeners to locate themselves within that sentiment. The hook repeats the phrase with subtle variations in vocal intonation, each time nudging the listener to reconsider: *Is it truly old, or is it simply familiar?* The simplicity of the language—four words—makes the title instantly memorable, yet its paradoxical nature forces deeper reflection each time the phrase resurfaces.

The opening line, often delivered in a whisper, sets the tonal expectation that the song will be an intimate confession rather than a bombastic confession. The hook’s melodic contour rises gently, then falls back to its original register, mirroring the emotional cycle of hope rising, seeing a glimpse of something new, and then slipping back into the comfort of the known.

## Production, Soundscape, and Mood

Axisa deliberately pairs lyrical introspection with a **sonic palette that feels deliberately aged**. The track opens with a low‑fi vinyl crackle, instantly conjuring the auditory texture of an older recording. This auditory cue primes the brain to hear the rest of the instrumentation through a nostalgic filter. 

The synths themselves are reminiscent of 80s new‑wave pads—warm, slightly detuned, and enveloped in reverb. This *retro synth ambience* serves two functions: first, it musically reinforces the lyrical focus on “old”; second, it creates a sense of *spatial distance*, as though the narrator’s voice is echoing in a larger, almost empty room. The sparse percussion, a brushed snare with a soft, ticking quality, resembles the ticking of a clock—an audible reminder of time’s passage.

Layered vocal harmonies appear only in the chorus, slightly detuned to mimic the effect of two voices recorded on an old cassette. The production’s intentional “lo‑fi” imperfections add an emotional texture that feels human and vulnerable, counterbalancing the polished synths with a raw edge. This juxtaposition mirrors the song’s thematic conflict: the desire for something polished and new, set against an underlying current of decay and wear.

## How Fans Commonly Interpret the Song and Why It Resonates

Among listeners, “It All Feels Old” has become a kind of *emotional shorthand* for moments when a relationship feels comfortable yet stagnant. Fans often report playing the track during late‑night drives, when they’re reflecting on long‑term partnerships that have settled into routine. The song’s ambiguity—never naming a specific lover or event—allows listeners to project their own histories onto the canvas, making the track personal for a diverse audience.

The resonance also stems from its *timeless production*; by using retro sonic elements, Axisa creates a sense that the song could have existed a decade ago or will still feel appropriate in the future. This timelessness aligns with the lyrical claim that everything feels old: the track itself becomes a meta‑commentary on how music can feel both fresh and nostalgic, mirroring the very phenomenon it describes.

Another factor driving fan connection is the *quiet optimism* embedded in the arrangement. Even as the verses descend into a near‑whisper, the chorus lifts slightly, and the final outro gently fades out rather than ending abruptly. Listeners interpret this as a subtle promise: the cycle might continue, but there’s always room for a subtle shift, a small change that can ripen into a new emotional season.

## FAQ

**Q: What does “old” refer to in the song?**  
A: “Old” functions as a metaphor for any experience that has been filtered through memory, routine, or repeated exposure. It isn’t limited to age; it captures the feeling that something once vivid has become a comfortable, almost stale backdrop.

**Q: Is the song about a romantic relationship or can it apply to other bonds?**  
A: While the lyrical imagery leans toward intimacy—mentions of shared spaces and personal objects—the core emotional conflict—comfort versus stagnation—can apply to friendships, family dynamics, or even a personal relationship with one’s own past.

**Q: Why does the production include lo‑fi vinyl crackle and retro synths?**  
A: Those sonic choices reinforce the lyrical focus on “old.” The crackle evokes a sense of time‑worn media, while the synth tones echo a bygone musical era, making the listener feel as though they are hearing something that has already lived a life.

**Q: Does the song suggest that the narrator is hopeless about change?**  
A: Not entirely. The song’s structure—quiet verses contrasted with slightly lifted choruses—implies a lingering, albeit subdued, hope. The hope is expressed through incremental musical lifts rather than a dramatic climax, suggesting change may come slowly.

**Q: How should listeners interpret the recurring light‑through‑curtains image?**  
A: The filtered light acts as a symbol for partial insight. It shows that clarity is present, but it is tempered by layers of history and memory, indicating that understanding the situation is possible, yet never fully unobstructed.

**Q: What makes “It All Feels Old” relevant across different listener demographics?**  
A: Its universality lies in the experience of routine becoming comfortable yet limiting—a feeling many encounter regardless of age or cultural background. The lyrical ambiguity lets each listener insert their own story, while the retro‑infused production provides a familiar soundscape that feels both nostalgic and fresh.  

**Q: Is there a hidden narrative beyond the surface lyric?**  
A: The song can be read as a commentary on artistic creation itself—how an artist may feel that every new piece riffs on previous work, making fresh output feel “old.” This meta‑reading aligns with the production’s deliberate homage to past styles, suggesting the track reflects both personal and creative cycles.
