The lingering ache that rides behind the line “things were better then a sickness” feels like a confession whispered from a hallway that has seen too many seasons of loss. Joshua Kemble’s *Things Were Better Then A Sickness* takes that fragile sentiment and threads it through a narrative that is at once personal and universal, inviting listeners to sit with the uncomfortable truth that nostalgia is often a mask for pain. The song does not simply reminisce; it interrogates why we cling to the idea of “better” when the past is colored by the very trauma we are trying to outrun. By unpacking the emotional layers, the recurring motifs, and the way the music itself mirrors the lyrical tension, we can begin to understand how Kemble transforms a simple phrase into a meditation on memory, denial, and the slow reclamation of self.

## Key Takeaways
- **Nostalgia as a defence mechanism:** The lyricist frames “better” not as an idealised past but as a refuge from an ongoing internal illness.  
- **The narrator’s conflict between longing and self‑preservation:** A tug‑of‑war emerges between wanting to return to what felt safe and recognizing that safety was illusory.  
- **Metaphoric illness:** “Sickness” operates on multiple levels—emotional, relational, and even societal—underscoring how trauma becomes a persistent disease.  
- **Production as emotional echo:** Sparse instrumentation, reverb‑drenched vocals, and subtle crescendos echo the feeling of being stuck in a memory loop.  
- **Listener resonance:** Fans interpret the track as a soundtrack for moments of grieving, addiction recovery, or the end of a toxic relationship, because the core feelings are broadly relatable.  

## The Emotional Core of the Song
At its heart, *Things Were Better Then A Sickness* is a portrait of a narrator caught in the amber of yesterday while the present gnaws with a quiet, relentless ache. The initial verses convey a yearning for a time that, on the surface, appeared calmer—a period before the “sickness” took hold. This longing is not about missing simple pleasures; it is about missing a version of the self that felt whole before fragmentation set in. The emotional register shifts in the chorus, where the repeated hook becomes a mantra, turning hope into a kind of self‑inflicted pressure. The narrator is simultaneously **desperate to hold onto the comfort of the past** and **aware that the past is a construct built around an unresolved wound**, generating a paradoxical grief that is both nostalgic and self‑critical.  

The song’s emotional arc mirrors the stages of grief: denial in the first lines, acknowledgment of the illness as the narrative progresses, and finally an uneasy acceptance that the “better then” may never be reclaimed. Kemble’s vocal delivery—soft yet edged with a strained tremor—underscores that the narrator is not merely recalling memories but *living* them anew each time the chorus repeats, like an echo that never fully fades.

## Main Themes and Message
### Memory as a Double‑Edged Sword  
Kemble treats memory not as a reliable archivist but as a selective storyteller. The repeated juxtaposition of “better” against “sickness” suggests that we often romanticise times that were actually rife with unseen struggles. The song asks listeners to consider whether the “better” they crave is a genuine longing or a **psychological coping strategy** that hides the pain of confronting current realities.

### The Illness Metaphor  
The term “sickness” is deliberately ambiguous, allowing it to stand in for mental health challenges, substance dependence, or any toxic relational dynamic. By never naming the specific ailment, Kemble amplifies the universality of the experience. The sickness is **internal**, insinuating that the protagonist’s battle is fought within their own mind, yet it also carries an external weight, suggesting that the environment (family, community expectations) can act as a vector for this disease.

### The Cycle of Return  
A subtle undercurrent throughout the track is the concept of **being pulled back** into the past whenever the narrator attempts to move forward. This is reflected in the lyrical looping and musical motifs that circle back to earlier phrases, reinforcing the idea that trauma can create a cyclical pattern where progress feels like a brief detour rather than a linear path.

## Symbolism and Metaphors
The lyrical landscape of *Things Were Better Then A Sickness* is peppered with visual and tactile symbols that deepen its meaning.  

- **The hallway:** Frequently referenced as a conduit between past and present, it represents a transitional space where memories gather dust but remain accessible. Walking down that hallway becomes a metaphor for **re‑entering old emotional territories**.  
- **Weather imagery:** Subtle nods to rain or overcast skies provide a backdrop for the internal gloom, suggesting that the narrator’s current emotional climate is never fully clear, mirroring the confusion that follows a prolonged illness.  
- **Glass or mirrors:** When the narrator sees their reflection “cracked,” it illustrates **fractured self‑perception**, a common symptom of prolonged psychological distress. The breakage hints at both vulnerability and an opportunity to see the whole in fragmented pieces.  

These symbols work together to create a **psychic map** that listeners can navigate in their own experiences, turning abstract emotional states into concrete images that carry emotional weight.

## The Role of the Title and Hook
The title functions as both a statement and a question. By positioning “things were better” *then* a “sickness,” Kemble forces the audience to interrogate the timeline of the illness—*when* did it begin, and *how* does it influence the notion of “better”? The hook—repeating the phrase with a melodic rise—acts as a **musical reminder** that the nostalgia is as haunting as it is comforting. The rise and fall within the hook mimic the breath of someone who is both remembering and choking on the recollection, reinforcing the physical sensation of yearning for a healthier past while being suffocated by its memory.

## Production Choices as Emotional Amplifiers
Beyond the lyrics, the arrangement deepens the song’s meaning. The opening strip of minimal acoustic guitar resolves into a low‑frequency rumble that feels like a heartbeat, underscoring the **vulnerability** of the narrator’s internal world. As the song progresses, layers of ambient synths rise like fog, slowly enveloping the vocal line, mirroring how the “sickness” can become an inescapable atmosphere.  

A strategic use of reverb on certain lines creates a feeling of distance, as if the singer is speaking from a room that is both close and far away—a sonic metaphor for the **emotional distance** between the present self and the remembered self. The restraint in percussion—a gentle, almost ticking rhythm—serves as a metronome for the passage of time, reminding listeners that while memories may feel timeless, they are measured against an ever‑advancing present.

## Fan Interpretations and Resonance
Listeners often bring personal narratives onto the track, interpreting the “sickness” through the lens of their own wounds. For some, the song becomes an anthem of recovery from **addiction**, where the “better then” refers to a life before dependence took hold. For others, it is a commentary on **toxic family dynamics**, where the past appears safer despite emotional manipulation that later surfaces as a lingering trauma.  

The widespread resonance lies in the **dual recognition** that the song validates both the yearning for a simpler past and the necessity of confronting that very simplicity’s flaws. Fans articulate that the track helps them articulate an internal conversation that previously existed only in fragments. By providing language for these feelings, Kemble’s song becomes a therapeutic mirror, allowing listeners to see their own cyclical longing and, importantly, to notice that the “sickness” is a shared human condition rather than an isolated flaw.

## FAQ

**Q: What does the “sickness” in the title actually represent?**  
A: It is a deliberately open‑ended metaphor that can stand for any internal affliction—mental health struggles, addiction, or a toxic relational pattern. Its ambiguity lets each listener map the concept onto their own experience.

**Q: Is the song advocating that we should forget the past?**  
A: Not at all. The track points out that idealising the past can be a form of avoidance. It encourages recognition of the past’s complexities rather than a wholesale dismissal or an uncritical glorification.

**Q: How does the musical arrangement reinforce the lyrical themes?**  
A: Sparse instrumentation reflects vulnerability, while the gradual layering of ambient sounds mimics the way trauma can slowly envelop consciousness. Reverb‑heavy vocals create distance, symbolising emotional separation from earlier self‑versions.

**Q: Why does the chorus feel so repetitive, and what purpose does that serve?**  
A: The repetition mirrors the cyclical nature of rumination—how thoughts about “better times” and the “sickness” spin in loops. It emphasizes the persistence of the narrator’s internal conflict.

**Q: Can the song be interpreted as a commentary on societal issues?**  
A: While primarily personal, the illness metaphor can extend to collective experiences—cultural stigmas surrounding mental health, generational trauma, or systemic neglect—making the track relevant beyond individual narratives.

**Q: What lyrical symbols are most crucial for understanding the song’s meaning?**  
A: The hallway (transition between past and present), weather (emotional climate), and broken mirrors (fractured identity) are key images that externalise internal states, helping listeners visualise abstract feelings.

**Q: How might the song guide listeners toward personal growth?**  
A: By articulating the tension between nostalgia and reality, it invites listeners to confront their own “sickness” rather than hide behind idealised memories, opening space for acknowledgement and, eventually, healing.
